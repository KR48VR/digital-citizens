How to use AI for digital citizens

Digital citizens are AI stand-ins for real people. The hope is to ask them how different groups would respond to a product, strategy or policy, instead of running a full survey each time. We tested whether that works by checking them against what real people actually said.

The short answer

AI cannot replace asking real people, but it can stretch a small survey a long way. Ask a few hundred people your question. Their answers alone are shaky for any group with only a handful of people in the survey, so AI fills in each group's answer with what many similar people said in a large public survey. Across the groups we looked at, the result was typically about as accurate as a survey twice the size.

The gain is biggest for small groups.

Two kinds of AI can fill in. One is a chatbot answering in place of people; we call it the chatbot kind. The other, record citizens, learns from real people's survey answers. For a brand-new question the two did about as well as each other, and best together. If your question has already been asked in large surveys, record citizens beat the chatbot.

Before you rely on it.

  • We tested attitude and values questions, such as trust and religion, in Singapore and the United States; not products or prices.
  • In our tests, the few hundred people and the public survey they were matched to came out of the same survey, so they lined up perfectly. A new survey run by a different firm may line up less well. The chatbot may also have read these surveys' published results, which flatters it.
  • Singapore's public survey dates from 2019 to 2020, so views that have shifted since will not show up until it is refreshed.

So run one pilot on your own topic first (step 5 under "What to do").

Two kinds of digital citizen, shown with one person

The chatbot kind

A chatbot is asked how a kind of person would answer, for example a 62-year-old Singaporean woman, or how a whole group would. It only knows what it has read about people like her in general, so everyone of the same age, sex and country gets the same answer.

Record citizens

We start from a real person instead: an anonymous 62-year-old woman who answered a large public survey (in Singapore, the World Values Survey: 2,012 people, about 290 questions each). Her answers, such as how often she goes to religious services, tell us how she is likely to answer your new question.

To learn that pattern, AI needs some people who answered both your question and the public survey's questions. For a brand-new question, those are the people in your own survey, which is why your survey also asks about 20 of the public survey's questions. The AI then applies the pattern to her and thousands of others, and adds up the results by group. This AI is a statistical model that learns patterns, not a chatbot.

Why it matters. Two 62-year-old women can hold very different views. The chatbot gives them the same answer. Record citizens give them different answers, because what they said before differs. Across thousands of people, that keeps the real differences between groups. Without it, groups look more alike than they really are, which is the main way digital citizens fail. On its own the chatbot was only rough; blended with a real survey it still helped, likely because the survey supplies the real differences between groups.

Where record citizens learn from

Past surveys. The World Values Survey, a public survey, asks the same questions in dozens of countries. That gave about 90,000 people in 61 other countries to learn from, and a computer can learn from all of them in under half an hour. In an earlier test, learning from 61 countries made it noticeably more accurate than learning from 2.

Your own small survey. A question about a new product or policy won't have been asked anywhere. Then the AI learns the same pattern from the few hundred real people you ask. For that, your survey also needs some of the big survey's standard questions, so your respondents can be linked to the records. Our tests used about 80. About 20 did almost as well in Singapore ({{v:singapore|+ record citizens (own survey, 20 standard questions)|300}} against {{v:singapore|+ record citizens (own survey)|300}} points off with 300 people) and a little less well in the United States.

What to do

Five steps. Numbers are points off for a typical group, for attitude questions like the ones we tested.

  1. 1Do you need answers for groups of people, or for individual people?

    • Individual peopleEven people themselves give the same answer only 70 to 80% of the time when asked again weeks later, so no AI can predict one person reliably. Ask the people themselves.
    • GroupsGo to step 2.

  2. 2Can you survey at least a few hundred real people?

    • NoThen rely on what already exists, and only if your exact question was in a large public survey. Its latest answers were {{v:us|last round's answers alone, no survey|300}} points off in the United States. Record citizens learned from other countries were {{v:singapore|record citizens alone, no survey|300}} in Singapore, between good enough and rough. For a brand-new question, AI alone is the rough level only: run a survey.
    • YesGo to step 3.

  3. 3Has your question been asked before in a large public survey? Check its question list, such as the World Values Survey's for Singapore.

    • Yes, in the latest round of a public survey that repeatsBlend your survey with its latest answers: {{v:us|+ last round's answers|300}} points off with 300 people in the United States.
    • Yes, but only in other countriesBlend in record citizens learned from those countries; the chatbot adds nothing: {{v:singapore|+ record citizens|300}} with 300 people in Singapore.
    • No, it is brand newAdd about 20 of the public survey's questions to your questionnaire (we picked the 20 that best stand in for the rest), so AI can match your people to the public survey. Then blend in record citizens learned from your survey and the best chatbot available (in our tests, Claude Opus): {{v:singapore|+ record citizens (own survey) + chatbot kind|300}} with 300 people in Singapore, {{v:us|+ record citizens (own survey) + chatbot kind|300}} in the United States. These are for attitude questions; for products and prices, the pilot in step 5 tells you. The extra questions make the questionnaire longer, so this pays off when they cost less than the people saved.

  4. 4Will the groups you care about have fewer than about 100 people each in your survey?

    • YesThis is where AI pays off: typically about {{tree:gain_small}} points closer than the survey alone (across survey sizes of 150 to 600).
    • NoAI adds little: typically about {{tree:gain_large}} points, about the same as the survey alone. A survey without AI may be enough.

  5. 5Is this the first time you are using AI on this topic?

    • YesSurvey 600 people once. Let AI fill in each group's answer from 300 of them and check it against what the other 300 said, as we did; do the same with the 300 alone. If AI lands clearly closer for the groups you care about, later surveys on this topic can use 300 people plus AI. With only 300 to spend, you can still use AI, but you are relying on our tests of attitude questions.
    • NoUse what your pilot showed.

Two more rules

  • Don't borrow numbers from different surveys or published reports. In separate tests they were typically 8 to 11 points off. The one exception is your exact question in the latest round of the same public survey (steps 2 and 3).
  • Insist on proof. Any digital citizens, ours or a vendor's, should show how many points off they are for each group, checked against real answers and compared with the survey alone.

How we know

Everything above comes from one test on two large real surveys, unless marked as a separate test: the World Values Survey in Singapore (2,012 people) and the General Social Survey in the United States (about 4,000 people).

1. DrawPick 150, 300 or 600 people at random from the large survey, ten times over.
2. Fill inWork out each group's answer from those people alone (the survey alone), or blended with AI, which counts as if it were extra people: more of them the better its track record. To treat a question as brand new, AI only sees its answers from the people picked.
3. CheckCompare each answer with what the people not picked actually said, and count the points off.
How AI fills in each group's answer: the blend

For each group, three voices are blended: the group's own people in your survey, record citizens, and the chatbot. Each AI's say is set by how accurate it had been on other questions, like weighing advisers by their track record. The real people's say grows with how many of them you asked. In Singapore, with a survey of 300, record citizens counted as much as about {{blend:rec}} real people per group, and the chatbot as about {{blend:chat}}.

So what. For small groups, AI does most of the work, which is why it helps them most. That much weight on AI is earned, not assumed: it comes from the AI's accuracy on other questions, and every blended answer on this page is checked against what real people said.

Why we score groups, not individual people

People don't fully agree with themselves. In a Stanford study, 1,052 Americans answered the same survey questions twice, two weeks apart, and on average 81% of their answers matched. In another US study we checked, {{self:people}} people matched {{self:match}}% of their answers a few weeks later, and only {{self:match_6_7}}% on questions with six or seven choices. So no AI can predict one person much better than that. Across a group, though, the changes mostly cancel out. In that study, the share giving each answer moved by only {{self:shift_group}} points for a typical group, well under the 3 points we treat as good enough to act on, though more for small groups. That is why we judge digital citizens by group answers.

The smaller the survey, the more AI adds

So what. In Singapore, AI cut the error by {{pct:singapore|+ record citizens (own survey) + chatbot kind|150}}% with 150 people, {{pct:singapore|+ record citizens (own survey) + chatbot kind|300}}% with 300 and {{pct:singapore|+ record citizens (own survey) + chatbot kind|600}}% with 600. In the United States, 300 people with AI did even better than 600 alone, but that comparison flatters AI because of how the US survey is designed; Singapore's is the fairer guide.

What each kind of AI adds, with a survey of 300

Each cell shows how many points off a survey of 300 lands for a typical group, and underneath, how much less error that is than the survey alone. For a brand-new question, neither kind of AI was clearly better than the other on its own. Together they did better than either alone with 150 people in both countries, and with 300 people in the United States. In the United States, answers to the same attitude questions from two years earlier were as accurate as anything else we tried, even with no new survey.

Why the United States looks even better: the US survey gives some people's answers more weight so that the sample matches the population. That makes 600 US answers only about as precise as 330 unweighted ones, so the survey alone looks weaker there.

Small groups gain the most

A survey of 300 Singaporeans holds about {{ppl:CHI|300}} Chinese Singaporeans, but only about {{ppl:MAL|300}} Malay and {{ppl:IND|300}} Indian Singaporeans. An answer read straight off {{ppl:IND|300}} people is shaky; AI brings in what many similar people said.

So what. The fewer people a group has in your survey, the more AI adds; for the largest group it changes little. Asking extra people from a small group helps it by about a point without AI, but little once AI fills in, so with AI it is usually not needed.

The chatbot works better in the United States than in Singapore

Asked with no survey at all, the chatbot kind was {{v:us|chatbot kind alone, no survey|300}} points off in the United States and {{v:singapore|chatbot kind alone, no survey|300}} in Singapore. The likely reason is that a chatbot learns from what has been written, and Americans' views by group have been surveyed and argued over in English for decades; Singapore's much less. Record citizens start from what real people in each country said, so they do not have this problem.

More on the chatbot, including using several chatbots

Blended with a survey, the chatbot kind's estimate counted for as much as about {{worth:us}} real people per group in the United States, but only about {{worth:singapore}} in Singapore. That is partly because it had been less accurate in Singapore on other questions, and partly because US answers are adjusted to match the population, so each real answer counts for a little less. Part of its American advantage may also be that it had already seen these exact surveys.

We tried five chatbots. Blending them by track record did about as well as the best one alone, while a simple average of them did worse, clearly so in the United States. So use the best chatbot available.

Limits, and what to test next

  • What the pilot checks. Whether the result holds for your topic, whether about 20 of the public survey's questions are enough, and whether your survey company's results line up with the public survey.
  • AI cannot know local facts it was never shown. A recent local change shows up only when real people are asked.
  • Reactions to change are untested, such as how people respond to a different price or wording.
  • Records age. Rebuild record citizens as the public surveys are refreshed.
  • Not yet costed: the analyst time to set up record citizens for a new topic, and the cost of the longer questionnaire.
  • Other places need their own check. We tested Singapore and the United States.
  • Next: letting the chatbot read each person's record. A US study at Stanford suggests this helps.

Standout groups. Groups whose answers sit far from the national average (here, the quarter of group answers furthest from it) gained about as much from AI as other groups did, compared with the survey alone, with no detectable difference: {{SO_TXT}} in Singapore with 300 people and record citizens learned from other countries. Compared with giving every group the overall answer instead, standout groups would gain most, because that is where copying the average misses most. A real survey does not have that weakness, so the gain follows how few people a group has.
