How to use AI for digital citizens

Digital citizens are AI stand-ins for real people. The hope is to ask them how different groups would respond to a product, strategy or policy, instead of running a full survey each time. We tested whether that works by checking them against what real people actually said.

The short answer

AI cannot replace asking real people, but it can stretch a small survey a long way.

Ask a few hundred people your question. Their answers alone are shaky for any group with only a handful of people in the survey. AI makes those answers less shaky by filling in each group's answer with what many similar people have said: in general, or in their answers to specific questions in large public surveys, in the same country or others.

Across the groups we looked at, the result of this filling in was typically about as accurate as a survey twice the size.

The gain is biggest for small groups.

Two kinds of AI can do this filling in:

  1. The chatbot kind: a chatbot answers in place of people, based on what it has read about people like them in general.
  2. Record citizens: AI that learns from real people's answers to specific questions in large surveys.

The two kinds perform differently for different questions:

  1. If your question has already been asked in large surveys, record citizens beat the chatbot.
  2. For a brand-new question, the two did about as well as each other, and best together. They must be combined with a real survey, which we call blending: on its own the chatbot was only rough, and record citizens need your survey to learn from.

How to read the numbers. "Points off" is how far an estimate is from what people really said, in percentage points. If 40% of older Singaporeans support a policy and we estimate 45%, that is 5 points off. Lower is better. Groups are slices of the population, such as people over 60, Malay Singaporeans or young adults: we looked at 9 in Singapore and 6 in the United States. "A typical group" is the middle one: half of all groups land closer, half further off.

Two kinds of digital citizen, shown with one person

The chatbot kind

A chatbot is asked how a kind of person would answer, for example a 62-year-old Singaporean woman, or how a whole group would. It only knows what it has read about people like her in general, so everyone of the same age, sex and country gets the same answer.

Record citizens

We start from a real person instead: an anonymous 62-year-old woman who answered a large public survey (in Singapore, the World Values Survey: 2,012 people, about 290 questions each). Her answers, such as how often she goes to religious services, tell us how she is likely to answer your new question.

To learn that pattern, record citizens need some people who answered both your question and the public survey's questions. For a brand-new question, those are the people in your own survey, which is why your survey also asks about 20 of the public survey's questions. A statistical model, not a chatbot, then applies the pattern to her and thousands of others, and adds up the results by group.

Why it matters. Two 62-year-old women can hold very different views. The chatbot gives them the same answer. Record citizens give them different answers, because what they said before differs. Across thousands of people, that keeps the real differences between groups. Without it, groups look more alike than they really are, which is the main way digital citizens fail when used on their own. On its own, with no survey, the chatbot was only rough; blended with a real survey it still helped, likely because the survey supplies the real differences between groups.

Where record citizens learn from

Past surveys. The World Values Survey, a public survey, asks the same questions in dozens of countries. That gave about 90,000 people in 61 other countries to learn from, and a computer can learn from all of them in under half an hour. In an earlier test, learning from 61 countries made it noticeably more accurate than learning from 2.

Your own small survey. A question about a new product or policy won't have been asked anywhere. Then record citizens learn the same pattern from the few hundred real people you ask. For that, your survey also needs some of the big survey's standard questions, so your respondents can be linked to the records. Our tests used about 80. About 20 did almost as well in Singapore ({{v:singapore|+ record citizens (own survey, 20 standard questions)|300}} against {{v:singapore|+ record citizens (own survey)|300}} points off with 300 people) and a little less well in the United States.

What to do

A worked example

Your team wants to know how Singaporeans feel about a new policy, by age and by ethnic group. The question has never been asked before.

  1. Survey 300 people. Ask your question, plus about 20 questions from the World Values Survey, the large public survey for Singapore.
  2. Build record citizens. An analyst uses your 300 people to learn how answers to those 20 questions relate to your question, then applies that to the 2,012 people in the World Values Survey.
  3. Ask the best chatbot for each group's answer (in our tests, Claude Opus did best).
  4. Blend record citizens and the chatbot's answers with your survey, giving each the say its accuracy on other questions earned.

What you get. For a typical group, about {{v:singapore|+ record citizens (own survey) + chatbot kind|300}} points off, against {{v:singapore|survey alone|300}} from the 300 people alone, and about the same as surveying 600 people. For Malay and Indian Singaporeans, about {{grp:MAL|+ record citizens (own survey) + chatbot kind|300}} and {{grp:IND|+ record citizens (own survey) + chatbot kind|300}}, instead of {{grp:MAL|survey alone|300}} and {{grp:IND|survey alone|300}} from the 300 people alone.

Not yet known: the analyst time and cost to set this up for a new topic. Whether to run a pilot first: see step 5 below.

If the blend comes out closer, later surveys on this topic can use 300 people plus both kinds of AI. The first round saves nothing; the saving starts with the second.
    • No, it is a one-offSkip the pilot and follow the worked example. The risk is that your topic behaves differently from the attitude questions we tested, so treat the small-group answers with some caution.

Two more rules

  • Don't borrow numbers from different surveys or published reports. In separate tests they were typically 8 to 11 points off. The one exception is your exact question, worded the same, in the latest round of the same public survey (step 2): different surveys reach different people in different ways, which is why borrowed numbers miss.
  • Insist on proof. Any digital citizens, ours or a vendor's, should show how many points off they are for each group, checked against real answers and compared with the survey alone.

How we know

Everything above comes from one test on two large real surveys, unless marked as a separate test: the World Values Survey in Singapore (2,012 people) and the General Social Survey in the United States (about 4,000 people).

1. DrawPick 150, 300 or 600 people at random from the large survey, ten times over.
2. Fill inWork out each group's answer from those people alone (the survey alone), or blended with one or both kinds of AI, each getting more say the better it did on other questions. To treat a question as brand new, record citizens only see its answers from the people picked.
3. CheckCompare each answer with what the people not picked actually said, and count the points off.
How blending works

For each group, three voices are blended: the group's own people in your survey, record citizens, and the chatbot. Each kind of AI's say is set by how accurate it had been on other questions, like weighing advisers by their track record. The real people's say grows with how many of them you asked. In Singapore, with a survey of 300, record citizens counted as much as about {{blend:rec}} real people per group, and the chatbot as about {{blend:chat}}.

So what. For small groups, the two kinds of AI do most of the work, which is why they help them most. That much weight on them is earned, not assumed: it comes from their accuracy on other questions, and every blended answer on this page is checked against what real people said.

Why we score groups, not individual people

People don't fully agree with themselves. In a Stanford study, 1,052 Americans answered the same survey questions twice, two weeks apart, and on average 81% of their answers matched. In another US study we checked, {{self:people}} people matched {{self:match}}% of their answers a few weeks later, and only {{self:match_6_7}}% on questions with six or seven choices. So no AI can predict one person much better than that. Across a group, though, the changes mostly cancel out. In that study, the share giving each answer moved by only {{self:shift_group}} points for a typical group, well under the 3 points we treat as good enough to act on, though more for small groups. That is why we judge digital citizens by group answers.

The smaller the survey, the more both kinds of AI add

So what. In Singapore, blending in both kinds of AI brought a typical group {{gain:singapore|+ record citizens (own survey) + chatbot kind|150}} points closer with 150 people, {{gain:singapore|+ record citizens (own survey) + chatbot kind|300}} with 300 and only {{gain:singapore|+ record citizens (own survey) + chatbot kind|600}} with 600. In the United States, 300 people blended with both kinds of AI did even better than 600 alone. That comparison flatters the blend a little: the US survey counts some people's answers more than others, so its 600 answers are worth only about 330. Singapore's comparison is the fairer guide.

Small groups gain the most

A survey of 300 Singaporeans holds about {{ppl:CHI|300}} Chinese Singaporeans, but only about {{ppl:MAL|300}} Malay and {{ppl:IND|300}} Indian Singaporeans. An answer read straight off {{ppl:IND|300}} people is shaky; both kinds of AI bring in what many similar people said.

So what. The fewer people a group has in your survey, the more both kinds of AI add; for the largest group they change little. Asking extra people from a small group helps it by about a point if you read the survey alone, but little once both kinds of AI are blended in, so with them it is usually not needed.

The chatbot did better in the United States than in Singapore: why

Asked with no survey at all, the chatbot kind was {{v:us|chatbot kind alone, no survey|300}} points off in the United States and {{v:singapore|chatbot kind alone, no survey|300}} in Singapore. The likely reason is that a chatbot learns from what has been written, and Americans' views by group have been surveyed and argued over in English for decades; Singapore's much less. Record citizens start from what real people in each country said, so they do not have this problem.

Blended with a survey, the chatbot kind's estimate counted for as much as about {{worth:us}} real people per group in the United States, but only about {{worth:singapore}} in Singapore. That is partly because it had been less accurate in Singapore on other questions, and partly because US answers are adjusted to match the population, so each real answer counts for a little less. Part of its American advantage may also be that it had already seen these exact surveys.

We tried five chatbots. Blending them by track record did about as well as the best one alone, while a simple average of them did worse, clearly so in the United States. So use the best chatbot available.

Limits, and what to test next

  • Not yet tested on products or prices, only on attitude and values questions such as trust and religion, in Singapore and the United States.
  • Not yet tested with a separately run survey. In our tests the few hundred people were drawn from the same big survey that record citizens learned from, which makes them look better than they may be in practice; we have not measured by how much. The chatbot may also have read these surveys' published results.
  • Records age. Singapore's public survey dates from 2019 to 2020, so views that have shifted since will not show up until it is refreshed.
  • AI cannot know local facts it was never shown. A recent local change shows up only when real people are asked.
  • Reactions to change are untested, such as how people respond to a different price or wording.
  • Other places need their own check. We tested Singapore and the United States.
  • Next: letting the chatbot read each person's record. An outside study suggests it helps.

Because of these, run one pilot on your own topic first (step 5 under "What to do").

Standout groups. Groups whose answers sit far from the national average (here, the quarter of group answers furthest from it) gained about as much from record citizens as other groups did, compared with the survey alone, with no detectable difference: {{SO_TXT}} in Singapore with 300 people and record citizens learned from other countries. Compared with giving every group the overall answer instead, standout groups would gain most, because that is where copying the average misses most. A real survey does not have that weakness, so the gain follows how few people a group has.
