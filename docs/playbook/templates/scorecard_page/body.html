How to use AI for digital citizens

Digital citizens are AI stand-ins for real people. The hope is to ask them how different groups would respond to a product, strategy or policy, instead of running a full survey each time. We tested whether that works by checking them against what real people actually said.

The short answer

AI cannot replace asking real people, but it can stretch a small survey a long way.

Ask a few hundred people your question. Their answers alone are shaky for any group with only a handful of people in the survey. AI makes those answers less shaky by filling in each group's answer with what many similar people have said: in general, or in their answers to specific questions in large public surveys, in the same country or others.

Across the groups we looked at, the result of this filling in was typically about as accurate as a survey twice the size.

The gain is biggest for small groups.

Two kinds of AI can do this filling in:

  1. The chatbot kind: a chatbot answers in place of people, based on what it has read about people like them in general.
  2. Record citizens: AI that learns from real people's answers to specific questions in large surveys.

The two kinds perform differently for different questions:

  1. If your question has already been asked in large surveys, record citizens beat the chatbot.
  2. For a brand-new question, you need a survey. Without one, the chatbot alone was much less accurate, and record citizens have no answers to your question to learn from. With a survey, combining either kind of AI's estimates with the survey's answers (we call this blending) made the groups' answers more accurate, by about the same amount. Blending in both kinds was the most accurate of all.
What blending means, and how it works

Blending combines each group's answer from your survey with the estimate from one or both kinds of AI into a single answer: an average in which each one counts for a share.

The shares depend on two things. First, how many people from that group you asked: the fewer there are, the more the group's answer leans on the AI. Second, each kind of AI's track record. Our test covered {{nq:singapore}} questions in Singapore and {{nq:us}} in the United States. For each question, each kind of AI's share was set to whatever had worked best on the other {{nq:singapore|others}} ({{nq:us|others}} in the United States), judged against what real people said.

So what. That is why the two kinds of AI help small groups most. Their share is not taken on trust: every blended answer on this page was checked against what real people said.

What counts as good enough

Why about 3 points is good enough to act on. It is what a conventional survey delivers without either kind of AI: in our test, a real survey of 600 people had a median of {{v:singapore|survey alone|600}} points off in Singapore and {{v:us|survey alone|600}} in the United States, and surveys of that size are routinely used for decisions. In practice, that is close enough to see which way a group clearly leans, such as 60% for and 40% against, but not close enough to call a near-tie, such as 52% against 48%. A conventional survey has the same limit.

Comparing two methods. When two methods differ by less than about half a point, the difference is often just luck, so we call them about the same unless our check of the chance range shows the gap is bigger than luck.

Two more findings that do not change the advice

Letting the chatbot read each person's answers first: about as good as record citizens, not adopted yet

The chatbot is usually told only what kind of person, or which group, it is answering for. We also tried a mix of the two kinds of AI: the chatbot reads each real person's own answers in the large survey first, the answers record citizens learn from. For one woman in the Singapore survey, it read her answers to 57 other questions, such as how often she goes to religious services, and then guessed her answers to our 20 questions. We did this for {{chk:people}} people and added up the guesses by group. We call this the chatbot reading each person's answers. Unlike record citizens, it does not learn a pattern from people who answered your question; it fills the gap with what it has read about people in general. We tested it in Singapore only.

For individual people, a gain bigger than luck. In a separate trial with 100 people, its top guess matched what the person said {{chk:ind_rec}}% of the time, against {{chk:ind_demo}}% when it knew only their age, sex, ethnicity and similar facts. People themselves repeat only {{self:match}} to 81% of their answers, so no AI can be reliable for one person (see What to do, step 1).

So what. Its best result, {{v:singapore_reading|+ record citizens (own survey) + chatbot reading records|300}}, is a tenth of a point better than both kinds of AI, which could be luck, and it costs about US${{chk:cost}} in chatbot fees each time it reads about 2,000 people's answers for 20 questions, while record citizens need no chatbot. So we have not adopted it. A larger test would show whether the small gain is real, especially for very small surveys.

Has the chatbot simply remembered the published answers? Three checks found no sign

The Singapore survey we use, the World Values Survey, publishes its results, so a chatbot may have read them. If it were remembering the answers rather than working them out, it would look better here than on a truly new question. We checked both chatbots, the one used on the rest of this page and the one that read people's answers (above), in three ways, in Singapore only.

  1. We asked them directly. Told the survey's name and asked for its published figures, the two chatbots were {{chk:f_named}} and {{chk:named}} points off what the whole survey said, about the same as when we did not name the survey. Had they remembered the published figures, they would have been within about 1 point. The estimates used on the rest of this page were also {{chk:fable_nat}} points off.
  2. Famous against little-known questions. Remembering would help most on the questions whose results are most widely published, such as how important religion is. Record citizens cannot remember anything, so we used them as the yardstick for how hard each question is. Against that yardstick, neither chatbot did better on the 7 most famous questions than on the other 13. (One measure we had planned looked like a warning sign at first. But it was picking up that some questions are simply easier for any chatbot to guess; once we allowed for that, the sign went away. Details in the evidence for analysts.)
  3. We disguised the questions. With every question reworded, the answer choices in reverse order and the survey not named, both chatbots were as accurate as with the original questions. For example, the chatbot that read people's answers had its top guess right {{chk:dis_top}}% of the time both ways.

So what. Both chatbots appear to work the answers out rather than recall them, so the chatbot results on this page are unlikely to come from memory. Only questions asked after the chatbots were built can rule memory out completely (see what to test next).

Two kinds of digital citizen, shown with one person

The chatbot kind

A chatbot is asked how a kind of person would answer, for example a 62-year-old Singaporean woman, or how a whole group would. It only knows what it has read about people like her in general, so everyone of the same age, sex and country gets the same answer.

Record citizens

We start from a real person instead: an anonymous 62-year-old woman who answered a large public survey (in Singapore, the World Values Survey: 2,012 people, about 290 questions each). Her answers, such as how often she goes to religious services, tell us how she is likely to answer your new question. A statistical model, not a chatbot, works this out for her and thousands of others, and adds up the results by group.

So what. Record citizens keep the real differences between people; the chatbot does not. Two 62-year-old women can hold very different views: record citizens give them different answers, while the chatbot gives them the same one. Across thousands of people, that makes the chatbot's groups look more alike than they really are. That is likely why, with no survey, it was only at the rough level. Blended with a survey, which supplies the real differences, it still helped.

Where record citizens learn from

Past surveys. The World Values Survey, a public survey, asks the same questions in dozens of countries. That gave about 90,000 people in 61 other countries to learn from, and a computer can learn from all of them in under half an hour. In an earlier test, learning from 61 countries made it noticeably more accurate than learning from 2.

Your own small survey. A question about a new product or policy won't have been asked anywhere. Then record citizens learn the same pattern from the few hundred real people you ask. For that, your survey also needs some of the big survey's standard questions, so your respondents can be linked to the records. Our tests used all that were available: 76 in Singapore. About 20 did almost as well in Singapore ({{v:singapore|+ record citizens (own survey, 20 standard questions)|300}} against {{v:singapore|+ record citizens (own survey)|300}} points off with 300 people) and a little less well in the United States.

What to do

Answer these questions in order. Numbers are medians, as in the two cases above.

  1. 1Do you need answers for groups of people, or for individual people?

    • Individual peopleEven people themselves give the same answer only {{self:match}} to 81% of the time when asked again weeks later (see why we score groups), so no AI can predict one person reliably. Ask the people themselves.
    • GroupsGo to step 2.

  2. 2Was your exact question, worded the same, in the latest round of a large public survey in your country?

    • YesReuse those answers, with no new survey. In our US test, the same survey's answers from two years earlier were {{v:us|last round's answers alone, no survey|300}} points off its latest answers, better than the 3 points we treat as good enough to act on. Adding a survey of 300 barely changed that (see what each kind of AI adds).
    • NoGo to step 3.

  3. 3Was it asked in large surveys in other countries?

    • YesUse record citizens learned from those countries, blended with a survey. In Singapore, with no survey they were {{v:singapore|record citizens alone, no survey|300}} points off, about as good as a survey of 300 on its own. Blended with a survey of 300 they were {{v:singapore|+ record citizens|300}}, as good as a survey of 600 (see what each kind of AI adds). Blending in the chatbot as well did not help.
    • No, it is brand newYou will need a survey: with none, the chatbot alone was only at the rough level ({{v:us|chatbot kind alone, no survey|300}} to {{v:singapore|chatbot kind alone, no survey|300}} points off; see the chatbot on its own). Go to step 4.

  4. 4Will any group you care about have fewer than about 100 people in your survey?

    • YesBlend your survey with both kinds of AI (how to, below). The fewer people a group has, the more it gains: about {{tree:band_1}} points closer than the survey alone for groups of 30 or fewer, about {{tree:band_3}} for groups of 60 to 100 (see small groups gain the most). Go to step 5.
    • NoBoth kinds of AI add less, about {{tree:gain_large}} points closer (less in Singapore, more in the United States), so a survey alone may be enough.

  5. 5Will you survey this topic more than once?

    • YesRun a pilot first. Survey 600 people once. Blend 300 of them with both kinds of AI, and check each group's answer against what the other 300 said; check the 300 alone the same way. Judge across all groups together, because one small group is too few people to check on its own. If the blend comes out closer, later surveys on this topic can use 300 people blended with both kinds of AI, so the saving starts with the second survey.
    • No, it is a one-offSkip the pilot and blend your survey with both kinds of AI. Treat the small-group answers with some caution: your topic may behave differently from the attitude questions we tested.
How to blend your survey with both kinds of AI, step by step
  1. Survey a few hundred people. Ask your question, plus questions from the large public survey for your country (in Singapore, the World Values Survey), so that record citizens can link your people to its records. Our main results used all of them; about 20 did almost as well in Singapore (see where record citizens learn from).
  2. Build record citizens. An analyst uses your survey to learn how answers to those questions relate to your question, then applies that to everyone in the public survey.
  3. Ask the best chatbot for each group's answer. In our tests that was Claude Opus, by a small margin (see five chatbots compared). The chatbot results on this page come from Claude Fable 5.1, the chatbot we started with, which did about as well.
  4. Blend record citizens and the chatbot's answers with your survey (see what blending means).

Two rules that always apply

  • Don't borrow numbers from different surveys or published reports. Different surveys reach different people in different ways, so borrowed numbers miss: in separate tests they had a median of 8 to 11 points off (see earlier, separate tests). The one exception is your exact question, worded the same, in the latest round of a large public survey in your country (step 2).
  • Insist on proof. Any digital citizens, ours or a vendor's, should show how many points off they are for each group, checked against real answers and compared with the survey alone.

How we know

Everything above comes from one test on two large real surveys, unless marked as a separate test: the World Values Survey in Singapore (2,012 people) and the General Social Survey in the United States (about 4,000 people).

1. DrawPick 150, 300 or 600 people at random from the large survey, ten times over.
2. Fill inWork out each group's answer from those people alone (the survey alone), or blended with one or both kinds of AI, each with a share set by its track record on the other questions (see what blending means). To treat a question as brand new, record citizens only see its answers from the people picked.
3. CheckCompare each answer with what the people not picked actually said, and count the points off.
Why we score groups, not individual people

People don't fully agree with themselves. In a Stanford study, 1,052 Americans answered the same survey questions twice, two weeks apart, and on average 81% of their answers matched. In another US study we checked, {{self:people}} people matched {{self:match}}% of their answers a few weeks later, and only {{self:match_6_7}}% on questions with six or seven choices. So no AI can predict one person much better than that. Across a group, though, the changes mostly cancel out. In that study, the share giving each answer moved by a median of only {{self:shift_group}} points across groups and questions, well under the 3 points we treat as good enough to act on, though more for small groups. That is why we judge digital citizens by group answers. Sources and details: people's own consistency.

The smaller the survey, the more both kinds of AI add

So what. In Singapore, blending the survey with both kinds of AI brought the median {{gain:singapore|+ record citizens (own survey) + chatbot kind|150}} points closer with 150 people, {{gain:singapore|+ record citizens (own survey) + chatbot kind|300}} with 300 and only {{gain:singapore|+ record citizens (own survey) + chatbot kind|600}} with 600 (how sure we can be: key comparisons). In the United States, 300 people blended with both kinds of AI did even better than 600 alone. That comparison flatters the blend: the US survey counts some people's answers more than others, to make its sample match the population, so its 600 answers are worth only about 330. Singapore's comparison is the fairer guide.

Small groups gain the most

A survey of 300 Singaporeans holds about {{ppl:CHI|300}} Chinese Singaporeans, but only about {{ppl:MAL|300}} Malay and {{ppl:IND|300}} Indian Singaporeans. An answer read straight off {{ppl:IND|300}} people is shaky; both kinds of AI bring in what many similar people said.

So what. The fewer people a group has in your survey, the more both kinds of AI add; for the largest group they change little. Malay and Indian Singaporeans went from the rough level ({{grp:MAL|survey alone|300}} and {{grp:IND|survey alone|300}} points off) to about {{grp:IND|+ record citizens (own survey) + chatbot kind|300}}, close to good enough to act on. The chatbot did better in the United States than in Singapore: why

Asked with no survey at all, the chatbot was {{v:us|chatbot kind alone, no survey|300}} points off in the United States and {{v:singapore|chatbot kind alone, no survey|300}} in Singapore. The likely reason is that a chatbot learns from what has been written, and Americans' views by group have been surveyed and argued over in English for decades; Singapore's much less. In a separate test it also reproduced far more of the real differences between groups in the United States than in Singapore (see group differences). Record citizens start from what real people in each country said, so they do not have this problem.

Blended on its own with a survey, the chatbot's estimate counted for about three times as much in the United States as in Singapore. That is partly because it had been less accurate in Singapore on other questions, and partly because each US answer counts for less, as explained under the chart above. Part of its American advantage may also be that it had already seen these exact surveys.

We tried five chatbots. Blended with a survey of 300, the best single one was Claude Opus ({{v:singapore|+ chatbot (Opus)|300}} points off in Singapore, {{v:us|+ chatbot (Opus)|300}} in the United States), just ahead of Claude Fable 5.1, the chatbot used for the results on this page ({{v:singapore|+ chatbot kind|300}} and {{v:us|+ chatbot kind|300}}). Blending all five by track record did about as well as the best one alone, while a simple average of them did worse, clearly so in the United States. So use the best chatbot available.

Limits, and what to test next

What we have not shown yet

  • Not yet tested with a separately run survey. In our tests, the few hundred people were drawn from the same big survey that record citizens are built from. That may make record citizens look better than they would be with a survey run separately; we have not measured by how much. The chatbot may also have read these surveys' published results, though checks in Singapore found no sign that it remembers them (see the memory checks).
  • Not yet tested on products, prices or reactions to change, such as a different price or wording; only on attitude and values questions such as trust and religion.
  • How to set each kind of AI's share on a new topic. Our test set the shares using other questions whose real answers were known. On your own topic, a pilot (What to do, step 5) gives you such answers to set and check them on; for a one-off survey you would have to borrow the shares from a test like ours, which we have not tried.
  • Set-up cost not yet known: the analyst time and cost to build record citizens for a new topic.
  • Records age. Singapore's public survey dates from 2019 to 2020, so views that have shifted since will not show up until it is refreshed.
  • Neither kind of AI knows recent local changes. They show up only when real people are asked, which is one more reason to keep a survey in the blend.
  • Other places need their own check. We tested Singapore and the United States.

What to test next

  1. A pilot on a real topic, with its own survey (What to do, step 5). It would show how much a separately run survey changes the results, what set-up costs, and how well the shares carry over to your topic. Until then, treat the blend's small-group answers with some caution.
  2. Questions asked after the chatbot was built, such as the next round of a large public survey, or the questions in your own pilot. Only these can rule out that the chatbot remembers published answers (see the memory checks).

Standout groups. Groups whose answers sit far from the national average (here, the quarter of group answers furthest from it) gained about as much from record citizens as other groups did, compared with the survey alone, with no detectable difference: {{SO_TXT}} in Singapore with 300 people and record citizens learned from other countries.
