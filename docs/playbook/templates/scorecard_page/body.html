How to use AI for digital citizens

Digital citizens are AI stand-ins for real people. The hope is to ask them how different groups would respond to a product, strategy or policy, instead of running a full survey each time. We built them two ways and checked each against what real people actually said.

The short answer

AI cannot replace asking real people, but it can stretch a small survey a long way. A few hundred real people answer your question. AI then fills in each group's answer. For a typical group, that is about as accurate as a survey twice the size.

The gain is biggest for small groups.

Two kinds of AI can fill in. The more common is a chatbot answering in place of people; we call this the chatbot kind. The other learns from patterns in real people's survey answers; we call the digital citizens built this way record citizens.

For a brand-new question, the two did about as well as each other, and best together, most clearly in smaller surveys. If your question has already been asked in large surveys, record citizens learned from those answers beat the chatbot, and adding the chatbot did not help.

Two ways to build a digital citizen, shown with one person

The chatbot kind

A chatbot is told something like "You are a 62-year-old Singaporean woman" and asked to answer in character. In our tests, it was asked directly for each group's answers.

It can only go on what it has read about people like her in general. So everyone in a group ends up sounding alike.

Record citizens

We start from a real person instead: an anonymous 62-year-old woman who answered a long survey about her views and values.

Her record does not include your new question, because nobody asked her. But her other answers, such as how often she goes to religious services or how much she trusts the government, tell us how she is likely to answer it.

This is where the AI comes in. It learns how people whose answers resemble hers tend to reply, from people who have answered both the new question and her other questions. Then it applies that to her. We do this for thousands of real people and add up the results by group. No chatbot is involved.

Where record citizens learn from

Past surveys. The World Values Survey, a public survey, asks the same questions in dozens of countries. That gave about 90,000 people in 61 other countries to learn from, and a computer can learn from all of them in under half an hour. In an earlier test, learning from 61 countries made it noticeably more accurate than learning from 2.

Your own small survey. A question about a new product or policy won't have been asked anywhere. Then the AI learns the same pattern from the few hundred real people you ask. For that, your survey also needs some of the big survey's standard questions, so your respondents can be linked to the records. Our tests used about 80.

Why the difference matters. Take two real 62-year-old women. One goes to religious services every week and trusts the government. The other never goes and distrusts it. The chatbot sees only their age, sex and country, so it gives them the same answer. As record citizens they get different answers, because what they said before differs. Groups differ partly because their members differ in ways like these, so across thousands of people, record citizens keep the real differences between groups. Working only from age, sex and country, a chatbot tends to make every group look alike, which is the main way digital citizens fail.

How we measure accuracy

The results that follow all come from one test, unless marked as separate. We took a large real survey and drew small surveys of 150, 300 and 600 people from it, ten times each. For each group, such as Malay Singaporeans or people over 60, we worked out its answer from the people drawn, either on their own (we call this the survey alone) or blended with AI estimates for that group. That is what we mean by AI filling in each group's answer.

How the blend works

For each group, three voices are blended: the group's own people in your survey, record citizens, and the chatbot. Each AI's say is set by how accurate it had been on other questions, like weighing advisers by their track record. The real people's say grows with how many of them you asked. In Singapore, with a survey of 300, record citizens counted as much as about {{blend:rec}} real people per group, and the chatbot as about {{blend:chat}}.

We then checked each answer against what the people not drawn actually said, and counted the gap in percentage points. If 40% of older Singaporeans really support a policy and we estimate 45%, that is 5 points off. We report the typical group: half of all group answers land closer than this, half further off.

Gaps under about half a point between methods are often within chance, so we call those about the same. The analysts' section gives the ranges.

Why we score groups, not individuals. People don't fully agree with themselves. In a Stanford study, 1,052 Americans answered the same survey questions twice, two weeks apart, and on average 81% of their answers matched. In another US study we checked, {{self:people}} people matched {{self:match}}% of their answers a few weeks later, and only {{self:match_6_7}}% on questions with six or seven choices. So no AI can predict one person much better than that. Across a group, though, the changes mostly cancel out. In that study, the share giving each answer moved by only {{self:shift_group}} points for a typical group, well under the 3 points we treat as good enough to act on, though more for small groups. That is why we judge digital citizens by group answers.

Which AI to add, and where it helps most

1. Which AI to add depends on one question: has yours been asked before?

  • Brand new, as most product and policy questions are. Record citizens learn from your own survey, with the chatbot kind added.
  • Asked in large surveys in other countries. Record citizens learn from those countries. The chatbot kind added nothing.
  • Asked recently in the same large survey. Reuse those answers. In the United States, answers to the same attitude questions from two years earlier were as accurate as anything else we tried, even with no new survey.

2. Small groups

A survey of 150 Singaporeans holds about {{ppl:CHI}} Chinese Singaporeans, but only about {{ppl:MAL}} Malay and {{ppl:IND}} Indian Singaporeans. An answer read straight off {{ppl:IND}} people is shaky. AI brings in what many similar people said, so the estimate no longer rests on a handful of people.

So what. The fewer people a group has in your survey, the more the AI adds. For the largest group it changes little.

3. Why the chatbot works better in the United States than in Singapore

Blended with a survey, the chatbot kind's estimate counted for as much as about {{worth:us}} real people per group in the United States. In Singapore it counted for only about {{worth:singapore}}. That is partly because it had been less accurate there on other questions, and partly because US answers are adjusted to match the population, so each real answer counts for a little less. Asked with no survey at all, it was {{v:us|chatbot kind alone, no survey|300}} points off in the United States and {{v:singapore|chatbot kind alone, no survey|300}} in Singapore: on its own, the rough level only, and in Singapore about the same as a 150-person survey alone ({{v:singapore|survey alone|150}}).

The likely reason is that a chatbot learns from what has been written. Americans have been surveyed continuously since the 1970s, and the results by age, race, religion and party are published and argued over constantly, in English. Singapore has far fewer such surveys, and its results by group are rarely written about, so the chatbot falls back on general impressions. Part of its American advantage may also be that it had already seen these exact surveys.

So what. Record citizens do not depend on what has been written, because they start from what real people in each country said. That matters most outside the United States. If you use a chatbot, use the best one available: we tried five, and blending them by track record did about as well as the best one alone, while a simple average of them did worse, clearly so in the United States.

Five rules for using AI for digital citizens

  1. Use AI to stretch a smaller real survey, not to replace it.

    Ask a few hundred people your exact question, plus about 20 standard questions from a large public survey so record citizens can be linked to them. AI then fills in each group's answer: for a typical group, about as accurate as a survey twice the size. In Singapore, 20 standard questions did almost as well as all 76 ({{v:singapore|+ record citizens (own survey, 20 standard questions)|300}} against {{v:singapore|+ record citizens (own survey)|300}} points off with 300 people), and a little less well in the United States.

  2. Pick the AI by asking whether your question has been asked before.

    Follow the three cases above. Most product and policy questions are brand new, which means record citizens learned from your own survey plus the chatbot kind. If you use a chatbot, use the best one available.

  3. AI helps most with small groups.

    In a 150-person survey, Indian Singaporeans went from {{grp:IND|survey alone}} points off to {{grp:IND|+ record citizens (own survey) + chatbot kind}}, while Chinese Singaporeans, the largest group, barely moved ({{grp:CHI|survey alone}} to {{grp:CHI|+ record citizens (own survey) + chatbot kind}}).

  4. Don't borrow numbers from other surveys or published reports.

    In separate tests, borrowing the most similar question from a different survey was typically 11 points off, and about 9 even where the wording was near-identical, because surveys reach different people, in different ways and years. About 100 published findings on Singapore, from research institutes and polling firms, were typically 8 points off, and adding them to record citizens and the chatbot did not help. The one exception is the same question in a recent round of the same large survey, as above.

  5. Insist on a scorecard.

    Any digital citizens, ours or a vendor's, should come with a scorecard like this one: how many points off they are for each group, checked against real people's answers, and how much less error that is than the survey alone. Ask especially about small groups, because that is where AI either earns its keep or fails. The test behind this page can be rerun on every new topic, with a pilot (see Limits).

Limits, and what to test next

  • Run a pilot on your own questions first. Every test used attitude and values questions from large surveys: trust, religion, politics, confidence in institutions. Run one pilot with 600 people. Let AI fill in the groups using only 300 of them, and compare that with what all 600 said. If the two agree, later surveys can use 300 people. The pilot also shows three things we could not test:
    • whether about 20 standard questions are enough for your topic;
    • whether a survey run by a different firm lines up with the records;
    • how much the chatbot adds on a truly new question, which it cannot have read about.
  • AI cannot know local facts it was never shown. It only reflects the data it learned from, so a recent local change shows up only when real people are asked.
  • Reactions to change are untested. How people respond to a different price or wording has not yet been checked against real experiments.
  • Records age. Singapore's survey was carried out from November 2019 to March 2020. Whether differences between groups hold up over time has not been tested, so record citizens should be rebuilt as surveys are refreshed.
  • Two countries tested in full. We tested the whole method in Singapore and the United States, and the chatbot kind in China too. Other countries, and cities within them, need their own check before use.
  • Next: letting the chatbot read each person's record. The chatbot kind was tested writing its estimates directly. Letting it read each real person's record first may do better. In the Stanford study, a chatbot told only people's age, race and similar facts got 71% of the way to their own consistency. Reading their other survey answers took it to 76%, and reading a two-hour interview with them to 85%.

Standout groups. Groups whose answers sit far from the national average (here, the quarter of group answers furthest from it) gained about as much from AI as other groups did, compared with the survey alone, with no detectable difference: {{SO_TXT}} in Singapore with 300 people and record citizens learned from other countries. Compared with giving every group the overall answer instead, standout groups would gain most, because that is where copying the average misses most. A real survey does not have that weakness, so the gain follows how few people a group has.
