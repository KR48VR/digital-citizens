How to use AI for digital citizens

Digital citizens are AI stand-ins for real people. The hope is to ask them how different groups would respond to a product, strategy or policy, instead of running a full survey each time. We built them two ways and checked each against what real people actually said.

The short answer

AI cannot replace asking real people, but it can stretch a small survey a long way. A few hundred real people answer your question. AI then fills in each group's answer, about as accurately as a survey twice the size.

The AI that stretches a small survey is the kind that learns from patterns in real people's past survey answers. We call the digital citizens built this way record citizens.

The gain is biggest for small groups.

The more common use of AI, a chatbot answering in place of people, helps less. It did better in the United States than in Singapore, and added nothing once record citizens were used. We call this the chatbot kind.

Two ways to build a digital citizen, shown with one person

The chatbot kind

A chatbot is told something like "You are a 62-year-old Singaporean woman" and asked to answer in character.

It can only go on what it has read about people like her in general. So everyone in a group ends up sounding alike.

Record citizens

We start from a real person instead: an anonymous 62-year-old woman who answered a long survey about her views and values.

Her record does not include your new question, because nobody asked her it. But her other answers, such as how often she goes to religious services or how much she trusts the government, tell us how she is likely to answer it.

This is where the AI comes in. It learns how people whose answers resemble hers tend to reply, from people who have answered both the new question and her other questions. Then it applies that to her. It can learn from two places.

Past surveys. The World Values Survey, a public survey, asks the same questions in dozens of countries. That gave about 90,000 people in 61 other countries to learn from, and a computer can learn from all of them in under half an hour. The wider it looks, the better it gets: learning from 61 countries instead of 2 made it noticeably more accurate.

Your own small survey. A question about a new product or policy won't have been asked anywhere. Then the AI learns the same pattern from the few hundred real people you ask. For that, your survey also needs some of the big survey's standard questions, so your respondents can be linked to the records. Our tests used about 80 of them; how few will do is not yet known.

We do this for thousands of real people and add up the results by group. No chatbot is involved.

Why the difference matters. Take two real 62-year-old women. One goes to religious services every week and trusts the government. The other never goes and distrusts it. The chatbot sees only their age, sex and country, so it gives them the same answer. As record citizens they get different answers, because what they said before differs. Across thousands of people, that keeps the real differences between groups. Without it, every group looks alike, which is the main way digital citizens fail.

How we measure accuracy. We draw a small survey, many times over, from a large real one. Each estimate is checked against what the people not drawn actually said, group by group, and we count the gap in percentage points. If 40% of older Singaporeans really support a policy and we estimate 45%, that is 5 points off. We report the typical group: half of all group answers land closer than this, half further off.

How much each kind of AI helps

Where AI helps most

Every result in this section comes from one test. We drew small surveys of 150, 300 and 600 people, ten times each, from a large real survey, and worked out each group's answer three ways:

  • Survey alone. Each group's answer is read straight off the people in your survey.
  • With the chatbot kind. Each group's answers are combined with the chatbot's guess for that group.
  • With record citizens. Each group's answers are combined with record citizens' estimate for that group.

When combining, the fewer people a group has in your survey, the more the AI's estimate counts. How much each kind of AI counts was set by how accurate it had been on other questions.

3. A brand-new question

The record citizens above learned from past surveys. In Singapore they learned from 61 other countries that asked the same questions; in the United States, from the same survey two years earlier. A question about a new product or policy has not been asked before, so record citizens must learn from your own survey instead. That works too, a little less well. In Singapore, 150 people with record citizens learned this way ({{SG_OWN_150}}) were about as accurate as 300 people alone ({{SG_ALONE_300}}), and 300 such people came close to 600 alone ({{SG_OWN_300}} against {{SG_ALONE_600}}). In the United States, 300 such people ({{US_OWN_300}}) beat 600 alone ({{US_ALONE_600}}).

4. Why the chatbot works better in the United States than in Singapore

The likely reason is that a chatbot learns from what has been written. Americans have been surveyed continuously since the 1970s, and the results by age, race, religion and party are published and argued over constantly, in English. Singapore and China have far fewer such surveys, and their results by group are rarely written about, so the chatbot falls back on general impressions. Asked on its own, with no survey, it reproduced 81% of the real differences between groups in the United States, 36% in Singapore and 25% in China. Part of its American advantage may also be that it had already seen these exact surveys. Record citizens do not depend on what has been written, because they start from what real people in each country said.

The takeaway

Five rules for using AI for digital citizens

  1. Use AI as record citizens, to stretch a smaller real survey.

    Real people still answer your question. Ask a few hundred people, plus a set of standard questions from a large public survey so record citizens can be linked to them. Record citizens then fill in each group's answer. In Singapore, 300 real people with record citizens were about as accurate for each group as 600 people alone, both {{SG_BOTH}} points off. That is the real upside. The same budget buys smaller surveys, or more frequent ones.

  2. Record citizens help most with small groups.

    In a 150-person survey, Indian Singaporeans went from {{IND_ALONE}} points off to {{IND_REC}}, and Malay Singaporeans from {{MAL_ALONE}} to {{MAL_REC}}, while Chinese Singaporeans, the largest group, barely moved ({{CHI_ALONE}} to {{CHI_REC}}). The bigger the survey, the less AI adds.

  3. Chatbots help less, and add nothing once you use record citizens.

    With 300 people, the chatbot kind cut the error by about a third in the United States and a sixth in Singapore; record citizens cut it by half and by more than a quarter. Adding the chatbot kind to record citizens made no difference. On its own, it reproduced 81% of the real differences between groups in the United States, but only 36% in Singapore and 25% in China.

  4. Don't borrow numbers from other surveys or published reports.

    We also tested using numbers that already exist, and they did not work. Borrowing the most similar question from a different survey was typically 11 points off, and about 10 even where the wording was near-identical, because surveys reach different people, in different ways and years. About 100 published findings on Singapore, from research institutes and polling firms, were typically 8 points off on their own, and adding them to record citizens did not improve the estimates.

  5. Insist on the scorecard.

    Any digital citizens, ours or a vendor's, should show how many points off they are for each group, checked against real people's answers, and how much less error that is than the survey alone. Ask in particular about small groups, because that is where AI either earns its keep or fails. The test behind this page can be rerun on every new question.

Limits, and areas for further experimentation

  • Check it on your own questions first. Every test used attitude and values questions from large surveys: trust, religion, politics, confidence in institutions. On a product, price or policy question, check that 300 real people plus record citizens still match 600 real people. Run one pilot with 600 people. Let record citizens fill in the groups using only 300 of them, and compare that with what all 600 said. If the two agree, later surveys can use 300 people.
  • AI cannot know local facts it was never shown. It sounds obvious, but it bears remembering. AI can only reflect the data it learned from, so a recent local change shows up only when real people are asked.
  • Reactions to change are untested. How people respond to a different price or wording has not yet been checked against real experiments.
  • Records age. Singapore's survey was carried out from November 2019 to March 2020. Whether differences between groups hold up over time has not been tested, so record citizens should be rebuilt as surveys are refreshed.
  • Two countries tested in full. We tested the whole method in Singapore and the United States, and the chatbot kind in China too. Other countries, and cities within them, need their own check before use.
  • The US test was the easier one. There, record citizens learned from the same survey two years earlier. Learning from other countries, as in Singapore, is harder and closer to most real uses.
  • Different survey firms were not tested. Our small surveys were drawn from the same survey as the records. A new survey, run by a different firm in a different way, may not line up as cleanly. The pilot will show it.
  • The chatbot kind may improve. It was tested writing its estimates directly. Letting it read each real person's record first may do better, and is the next test.
