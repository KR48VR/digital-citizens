Digital citizens · tested in the United States, Singapore and China · October 2026

How to use AI for digital citizens

Digital citizens are AI stand-ins for real people. The hope is to ask them how different groups would respond to a product, strategy or policy, instead of running a full survey each time. We built them two ways and checked each against what real people actually said.

The short answer

AI cannot replace asking real people, but it can stretch a small survey a long way. A few hundred real people give the overall answer. AI then works out how each group differs, about as accurately as a survey twice the size.

The AI that stretches a small survey is the kind that learns from patterns in real people's past survey answers. We call the digital citizens built this way record citizens.

The more common use of AI, a chatbot answering in place of people, is less reliable. It filled in the groups well in the United States. In Singapore it helped only with groups that stand out from the average. We call this the chatbot kind.

Two ways to build a digital citizen, shown with one person

The chatbot kind

A chatbot is told something like "You are a 62-year-old Singaporean woman" and asked to answer in character.

It can only go on what it has read about people like her in general. So everyone in a group ends up sounding alike.

Record citizens

We start from a real person instead: an anonymous 62-year-old woman who answered a long survey about her views and values.

Her record does not include your new question, because nobody asked her it. But her other answers, such as how often she goes to religious services or how much she trusts the government, tell us how she is likely to answer it.

This is where the AI comes in. It learns how people whose answers resemble hers tend to reply, from people who have answered both the new question and her other questions. Then it applies that to her. It can learn from two places.

Other countries. The World Values Survey, a public survey, asks the same questions in dozens of countries. That gave about 90,000 people in 61 other countries to learn from, and a computer can learn from all of them in under half an hour. The wider it looks, the better it gets: learning from 61 countries instead of 2 made it noticeably more accurate.

Your own small survey. A question about a new product or policy won't have been asked anywhere. Then the AI learns the same pattern from the few hundred real people you ask. For that, your survey also needs some of the big survey's standard questions, so your respondents can be linked to the records. Our tests shared about 76 questions; how few will do is not yet known.

We do this for thousands of real people and add up the results by group. No chatbot is involved.

Why the difference matters. Take two real 62-year-old women. One goes to religious services every week and trusts the government. The other never goes and distrusts it. The chatbot sees only their age, sex and country, so it gives them the same answer. As record citizens they get different answers, because what they said before differs. Across thousands of people, that keeps the real differences between groups. Without it, every group looks alike, which is the main way digital citizens fail.

How we measure accuracy. We compare each estimate with what real people actually said, group by group, and count the gap in percentage points. If 40% of older Singaporeans really support a policy and we estimate 45%, that is 5 points off.

So what. 300 real people with AI filling in the groups land under 3 points off for each group. So do 600 real people with no AI. That is good enough to act on, at half the survey size. So the same survey budget buys twice the detail by group, or surveys twice as often.

The gain is biggest for small groups, and for groups whose views stand out from the average. The next section shows both.

How much each kind of AI helps

Where AI helps most

The short answer said that real people give the overall answer and AI works out how each group differs. It also said the chatbot kind fills in groups well only in the United States. This section shows why, in three steps.

1. Small groups

A survey of 150 Singaporeans holds about 115 Chinese Singaporeans, but only about 18 Malay and 14 Indian Singaporeans. An answer read straight off 14 people is shaky. Record citizens bring in hundreds of real records of people like them, so the estimate no longer rests on a handful of people. In this test the chatbot kind was mixed in as well, but record citizens did most of the work.

2. Groups that stand out

Our 62-year-old woman belongs to several groups at once: women, people over 60, and her ethnic and religious group. On any one question, most groups answer close to the national average. A few standout groups answer very differently; here, the quarter of group answers furthest from the national average. Which groups stand out depends on the question and the country, but they are often an ethnic or religious minority, the young or the old, or people with more or less education. In our Singapore tests, Indian and Malay Singaporeans and young adults stood out most often. In the United States it was Republicans, Democrats and Black Americans. Decisions often turn on these groups.

To see how much each kind of AI helps, start from the real overall answer and get each group's answer three ways. These are the table's three columns:

  • No AI. Every group gets the overall answer, as if all groups thought alike.
  • The chatbot kind guesses how each group differs, from what it has read about people like them.
  • Record citizens predict how each group differs, from what real people in that group said before.

Each cell shows how many points off that way lands. Underneath is how much smaller that is than the No AI column. For example, for Singapore's standout groups, No AI is 7.6 points off and record citizens are 4.6 points off, so record citizens leave 40% less error.

So what.

  • In the United States, both kinds of AI help, and record citizens help more: they cut the error by 42% for most groups and 79% for standout groups, against 33% and 68% for the chatbot kind.
  • In Singapore, for most groups, No AI is already close, at 1.9 points, because these groups answer near the national average. The chatbot kind made it slightly worse. Record citizens cut the error by about a quarter.
  • For Singapore's standout groups, No AI misses by 7.6 points. Here both kinds of AI help a lot, each cutting the error by about 40%. This is where AI earns its keep.

3. Why the chatbot works better in the United States than in Singapore

The likely reason is that a chatbot learns from what has been written. Americans have been surveyed continuously since the 1970s, and the results by age, race, religion and party are published and argued over constantly, in English. Singapore and China have far fewer such surveys, and their results by group are rarely written about, so the chatbot falls back on general impressions. It reproduced 81% of the real differences between groups in the United States, 36% in Singapore and 25% in China. Part of its American advantage may also be that it had already seen these exact surveys. Record citizens do not depend on what has been written, because they start from what real people in each country said.

The takeaway

Five rules for using AI for digital citizens

  1. Use AI as record citizens, to stretch a smaller real survey.

    Real people still give the overall answer. Ask a few hundred people your exact question, plus a set of standard questions from a large public survey so record citizens can be linked to them. Record citizens then fill in the groups, with the chatbot kind weighted in by its track record. 300 real people with this AI were about as accurate for each group as 600 without AI, both under 3 points off. That is the real upside. The same budget buys smaller surveys, or more frequent ones.

  2. Record citizens help most with small groups and standout groups.

    In a 150-person survey, Indian Singaporeans went from 8.2 points off to 2.9, and Malay Singaporeans from 6.2 to 3.6. For standout groups, record citizens cut the error compared with No AI by 40% in Singapore and 79% in the United States, against about a quarter and 42% for most groups.

  3. Chatbots are much less useful, except in the United States.

    In the United States the chatbot kind cut the error by a third for most groups and two-thirds for standout groups. In Singapore it did not help most groups, though it helped standout groups about as much as record citizens. It reproduced 81% of the real differences between groups in the United States, but only 36% in Singapore and 25% in China.

  4. Don't borrow numbers from other surveys or published reports.

    We also tested using numbers that already exist, and they did not work. Borrowing the most similar question from a different survey was typically 11 points off, and about 10 even where the wording was near-identical, because surveys reach different people, in different ways and years. About 100 published findings on Singapore, from research institutes and polling firms, were typically 8 points off on their own, and adding them to record citizens did not improve the estimates.

  5. Insist on the scorecard.

    Any digital citizens, ours or a vendor's, should show how many points off they are for each group, checked against real people's answers, and how much error they remove compared with No AI. Ask in particular about small groups and standout groups, because that is where AI either earns its keep or fails. The test behind this page can be rerun on every new question.

Limits, and areas for further experimentation

  • Check it on your own questions first. Every test used attitude and values questions from large surveys: trust, religion, politics, confidence in institutions. On a product, price or policy question, check that 300 real people plus record citizens still equal 600 real people. Run one pilot with 600 people. Let record citizens fill in the groups using only 300 of them, and compare that with what all 600 said. If the two agree, later surveys can use 300 people.
  • AI cannot know local facts it was never shown. It sounds obvious, but it bears remembering. AI can only reflect the data it learned from, so a recent local change shows up only when real people are asked.
  • Reactions to change are untested. How people respond to a different price or wording has not yet been checked against real experiments.
  • Records age. Singapore's survey was carried out from November 2019 to March 2020. Whether differences between groups hold up over time has not been tested, so record citizens should be rebuilt as surveys are refreshed.
  • Three countries so far. We tested the United States, Singapore and China. Other countries, and cities within them, need their own check before use.
  • A simpler shortcut came close. In Singapore, filling in groups from age, sex, ethnicity and religion alone was about as close as record citizens on points off. Record citizens were better at getting the pattern of group differences right, and did better in the United States.
  • Different survey firms were not tested. Our small-survey tests drew people from the same survey as the records. A new survey, run by a different firm in a different way, may not line up as cleanly. The pilot will show it.
  • The chatbot kind may improve. It was tested writing its estimates directly. Letting it read each real person's record first may do better, and is the next test.
