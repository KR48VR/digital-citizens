Part 1

How we did this

The question King set: can AI stand in for real people when you want to know how different groups, in different countries, would respond to a product, strategy or policy, and how do we know it is accurate? Everything was tested against what real people actually said, on real public surveys, before any claim reached the page.

Two kinds of digital citizenThe chatbot kind answers in character. Record citizens predict each real person's answer from their record.

Score against the truthEach group's answer, with and without AI, is checked against the people not drawn: points off, and less error than the survey alone.

The tests, in the order we ran them

  1. Baselines. How close simple rules get: last wave's answers, the average of other countries, demographics alone.
  2. Chatbot stress test. Claude, standing in for the chatbot, predicted group answers blind in the US, Singapore and China. It reproduced 81%, 36% and 25% of the real differences between groups.
  3. Five fixes for flattening. Stretching the chatbot's differences, sharpening its answers and showing it real answers first did not help. Building citizens from real survey records did.
  4. Learning from more countries. Record citizens trained on 61 other countries, about 90,000 people, were more accurate than those trained on 2.
  5. Weighing sources by track record. King's superforecasting idea: record citizens earned about three times the chatbot's weight.
  6. Published findings as evidence. 96 findings were 8 points off on their own and did not improve the estimates.
  7. The survey library. King's idea: borrowing the most similar question from another survey was typically 11 points off, and about 10 for the 2 questions with near-identical wording.
  8. Small surveys, scored honestly. A re-check that corrected an earlier, too-flattering result: 300 real people plus record citizens are about as accurate for each group as 600 without AI.
  9. The United States, like for like. Starting from the real overall answer, record citizens filled in US groups more closely than the chatbot: 1.8 against 2.3 points off.
  10. Which groups stand out. Checked in the data: Indian and Malay Singaporeans and young adults most often in Singapore; Republicans, Democrats and Black Americans in the US.
  11. Independent audit. A helper agent recomputed every number on the scorecard from the raw results. Its biggest catch: the "standout groups" figures were really averages over all groups. Re-done properly, they showed the chatbot kind does not help most groups in Singapore but helps its standout groups about as much as record citizens. The page was rewritten to match (QA log, finding 24).
  12. One method for everything. King noticed the page mixed setups: the headline blended both kinds of AI, while the chatbot section used a different comparison. One new test now produces every headline number: small surveys drawn ten times, each group's answer worked out with and without each kind of AI in the same way, with 95% ranges. A second audit showed the US "past survey" result was really reuse of the same questions asked two years earlier. The final picture: AI still makes a survey about twice as valuable, the gain is biggest for small groups, and which AI to add depends on whether your question has been asked before. For a brand-new question, record citizens learned from your own survey plus the chatbot kind did best (QA log, findings 47 to 63).

How accuracy is measured

  • Points off: the gap between an estimate and the real answer, in percentage points per answer option, for each group.
  • Typical group: the middle result across all groups and questions. Half of all group answers land closer than this, half further off.
  • Survey alone: each group's answer read straight off the people in the survey. Every method is compared with it, as "less error".
  • Combining: each group's own answers are combined with an AI estimate; the fewer people a group has, the more the estimate counts. How much each kind of AI counts is fitted on other questions only.
  • The floor: about 1.3 points, how far a full national survey is from the true answer by chance alone. No method can be scored closer than that.
  • Hold-out scoring: a small sample is always scored against people not in it, and scores, not predictions, are averaged over repeat draws.

Three ways of working

King used Claude as a tool{{N_TOOL}}

King asked for a defined output, such as an explanation, an edit or a rewrite, and Claude produced it. The substance came from the request.

King worked with Claude as a teammate{{N_TEAM}}

Both shaped the result. King brought an idea, challenged a claim or drafted wording, or Claude pushed back, found a problem or proposed something that changed the plan.

Each worked independently{{N_IND}}

One worked without the other for a long stretch: Claude building, downloading or running experiments for up to an hour; King reviewing the page alone between rounds.

Part 4

Playbook and package

Everything needed to run a study like this again, for a new country, city or question, or to pick this one up later. The files live in the repository, where Claude Code loads the two skills automatically; copies are published with this page so King can read them here. The index is docs/playbook/README.md.

The two pages

  • How to use AI for digital citizens: the scorecard for a C-suite reader. Share it with anyone who should see the findings.
  • This casebook: the method, QA log, working record and package. Private.

The playbook in eight steps

  1. Frame the question: exact wording, and the groups the decision turns on.
  2. Find rich public records: surveys that publish every person's answers to many questions; note when they were collected.
  3. Build record citizens: learn the pattern from other countries, or from your own small survey with standard questions included.
  4. Measure with the scorecard: one test for every number; points off for a typical group, and how much less error than the survey alone, worked out from unrounded results.
  5. Pilot before relying on it: 600 people; record citizens fill in the groups from 300; compare with all 600.
  6. Decide with the five rules.
  7. Report for a C-suite reader: agreed terms, define before use, one running example, numbers with their meaning.
  8. QA before sharing: accuracy, language, accessibility, aesthetics; log every finding.
